Theorema Egregium · Dunkerque to Barcelona, 1792–1798

Measure the triangles. Gauss gives k, then R.

Delambre and Méchain's survey, read only from inside the surface. The lengths come from the Melun base, laid ruler by ruler. The curvature comes from the angles they observed, and from nothing else: no latitudes, no azimuths, no stars. Every number below is a raw printed value from the four volumes of Base du système métrique décimal (1806–1821), recomputed here. Steps marked worked out here are not in the volumes.

1 · The theorem

Gauss, Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas (1827), Art. 12:

"If a curved surface is developed upon any other surface whatever, the measure of curvature in each point remains unchanged."

Curvature \(k\) depends only on lengths and angles measured on the surface. A sphere has \(k=1/R^2\) everywhere; a plane has \(k=0\).

So a surveyor who never looks up can still find out whether the ground is curved, and how much. That is the only claim this page tests.

2 · Triangles

For a triangle of shortest lines on a sphere, the angles exceed 180° by the area over \(R^2\) (Art. 20):

$$A+B+C-\pi=\iint k\,d\sigma=\frac{\text{Area}}{R^{2}}$$

Area comes from the measured distances. The excess comes from the observed angles. Solve for \(R\).

A typical survey triangle is about 280 km². On a 6 371 km Earth its angles add up to 180° + 1.45″. The whole curvature signal is that 1.45″.

5 · The curvature, from angles alone

R = 5 360 km 1σ 5 160 – 5 590 km · 91 triangles · 25 900 km² · worked out here

The ground is clearly curved: the observed angles average 2.11″ over 180°, where a plane gives 0″. But the radius comes out 16% low. Each triangle closes 0.66″ too wide for a 6 371 km Earth, about 0.2″ per angle, and the bias grows southward into Méchain's mountain triangles. Fitting a constant bias per triangle absorbs 0.29″ and gives 5 700 km (1σ 5 200–6 300 km), which includes the true value.

Dot = least-squares fit of excess = area/R², whisker = 1σ from the closure scatter (1.8″ r.m.s. per triangle). Only triangles with all three angles observed are used; an angle marked * in Tome I was concluded from the other two, so its sum carries nothing. Uneven air bending sight lines sideways is the likely cause of the bias; that is a hypothesis, not tested here.

6 · k, the measure of curvature

k = 3.48 × 10⁻⁸ km⁻² 1σ 3.19 – 3.76 × 10⁻⁸ · modern 1/6 371² = 2.46 × 10⁻⁸ · worked out here

Gauss's measure of curvature is what the triangles actually give; R is only \(1/\sqrt{k}\). For one triangle, \(k_i=\varepsilon_i/\text{Area}_i\). For all 91 together, least squares on \(\varepsilon_i = k\,\text{Area}_i\):

$$k=\frac{\sum_i \text{Area}_i\,\varepsilon_i}{\sum_i \text{Area}_i^{2}},\qquad \sigma_k=\frac{s}{\sqrt{\sum_i \text{Area}_i^{2}}},\qquad R=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}$$

with \(\varepsilon\) in radians (1″ = 4.848 × 10⁻⁶ rad) and \(s\) = 1.80″, the r.m.s. scatter of the closures about the fit.

One triangle tells little: 2.15″ of triangle 12's 3.62″ is error. Single-triangle values run from −1.6 to +3.7 × 10⁻⁷ km⁻², and 11 of the 91 come out negative (angles summing to less than 180°). Only the fit over all of them is meaningful. With a constant bias fitted as well, k = 3.09 × 10⁻⁸ (1σ 2.50–3.68 × 10⁻⁸), which includes the modern value.

The units: excess in radians is dimensionless, so k has units of 1/area. 2.46 × 10⁻⁸ km⁻² is about one radian of excess per 41 million km², the area of a twelfth of the globe.

8 · Limits: what the theorem can and cannot show here

The Theorema Egregium gives local curvature k, nothing more. Read honestly, this survey's k is a weak indication of a sphere, for five reasons:

LimitIn this data
The radius deviates widely. The derived R is far from the accepted value, and the regions disagree with each other.5 360 km against 6 371 km: 1 010 km (16%) short, 4.6σ on the random error alone. North 5 880 km, centre 5 550 km, south 4 930 km: a 950 km (19%) spread. Single triangles give R from about 1 600 km to infinity, and 11 of 91 give negative k.
Signal below noise. One triangle's excess is smaller than its own angle error.Expected excess ≈ 1.45″ per triangle; closure scatter 1.80″ r.m.s.; systematic offset 0.66″. Only the average over 91 triangles shows curvature.
Constant k is not shown. A sphere needs the same k everywhere; this data cannot confirm that.The regional differences are 1.3–1.4σ: neither proven nor ruled out. The spread is as consistent with uneven curvature, or uneven error, as with one radius.
k > 0 is not unique to a sphere. Any positively curved surface gives positive excess.An ellipsoid, an egg, or a bulge on an otherwise different surface all fit. A 1 075 × 60 km strip along one meridian says nothing about the surface beyond the strip, or whether it closes on itself.
Oblateness is out of reach. On an oblate Earth k changes along the arc, but far too little.On today's ellipsoid k differs by 0.23% between 41° and 51° latitude; the measurement's 1σ is about 8%. The theorem here is about 35 times too coarse to see the flattening.

So the strongest claim the theorem supports is that this strip of France and Catalonia is, on average, positively curved by roughly Earth's amount, with a large and unexplained deviation in the radius. That it is a sphere, or an oblate spheroid, needs more than the triangles.

The 4.6σ figure counts only random error. Fitting a constant bias per triangle brings 6 371 km inside 1σ, but that is a model choice: it assumes the extra excess is error rather than curvature, which the angles alone cannot decide.

9 · Bias in the record: corrections toward an expected figure

The survey was not a test of the Earth's shape. Its goal was a metre fixed in advance as a ten-millionth of the quadrant, on an Earth already assumed to be an ellipsoid. Several corrections in the volumes move the data toward that expectation, and the volumes say so themselves:

CorrectionWhat was doneSource
Spherical anglesThe Tome II angles are the observed ones forced to close on an assumed sphere (mean degree 57 020 toises). Their excess returns the assumption, not a measurement.Tome II pp. 702, 706
Angles adjusted to make the bases agreeThe 29 cm Melun–Perpignan misclosure was removed by changing the angles of triangles 44–100 by 0.01–0.1″, "agissant toujours dans le même sens".Tome II pp. 704–705
Choice among observation series"J'avois cru pouvoir choisir parmi les différentes séries celles qui donnoient des angles moindres" (triangle 20); "en se permettant un choix entre les séries, on feroit évanouir l'erreur" (triangle 26). The commission's angles are "le résultat le plus probable", not every reading.Tome I pp. 518, 520
A latitude changed to fit the expected trendFrom Montjuïc to Formentera "le degré augmente au lieu de diminuer", so Delambre adds 1.62″ to Montjuïc's latitude to restore the southward decrease an oblate Earth predicts.Tome III p. 548
Pendulum formula built on a theoretical flatteningBiot's formula for gravity with latitude assumes Laplace's 1/307, fits only the Unst value, then compares the other stations against it.Tome IV p. 575

So the volumes' own results lean toward the figure they set out to find, and they cannot count as an independent test of it. For this report the effect is limited: the curvature fit uses the Tome I observed angles, before the sphere closure and the base adjustment. Those are still the commission's chosen means, so some selection is already inside them. It did not pull them toward the expected answer: they close 0.66″ wider than a 6 371 km Earth predicts.

A cleaner test would start from the individual repetition series in the station registers (Tome I pp. 11–510), before the commission chose among them. Méchain's private handling of his Barcelona latitudes, a discrepancy not published in his lifetime, is a well-documented part of the survey's history (Ken Alder, The Measure of All Things, 2002).

11 · Conclusion

Measured entirely from inside the surface, as the Theorema Egregium allows, the survey shows the ground is curved, but only on average and with a large deviation in the radius: R = 5 360 km (1σ 5 160–5 590 km), 1 010 km (16%) short of 6 371 km, with regions spread from 4 930 to 5 880 km. The theorem cannot show constant curvature, a closed sphere, or the flattening. The limit is the angles, not the distances.

The survey's reference frame comes from the stars as well. How many degrees each stretch spans, and so the flattening, rests on star latitudes at Dunkerque, Paris, Évaux, Carcassonne and Montjuïc; the chain's orientation rests on star azimuths; Clairaut's argument rests on the sidereal day. Even then the sections scatter, and Delambre had to add the Peru arc to adopt 1/334.

Modern geodesy leans on the sky even more. Chains of triangles like this one are no longer how national networks or the Earth's figure are measured; they were replaced by satellite methods (GNSS, satellite laser ranging, DORIS) and by VLBI. The International Terrestrial Reference Frame is built from those four techniques, and it is oriented against the International Celestial Reference Frame, the positions of distant quasars observed by VLBI (DGFI-TUM). Ground instruments still do local work, such as levelling and gravimetry, but a purely surface measurement of the Earth's shape, the kind the Theorema Egregium allows, is no longer part of how the figure is determined.

The record also carries its makers’ intent: angles, a latitude and a gravity formula were adjusted toward the figure the survey was built to deliver. It confirms that expectation more than it tests it.

Each of these is a fallible, inductive measurement, good to a stated sigma. Together, the triangles give the lengths and the curvature, the stars give the latitudes and orientation, and the pendulum gives gravity. They point to an oblate spheroid; no one of them alone shows it.
